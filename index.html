Surgical Technologist · Sterile field, instruments, and anticipating the surgeon's next move

A surgical tech keeps the sterile field safe, manages instruments and supplies, and tracks fluids, counts, and timing. Behind those tasks is calculus: derivatives measure how fast things change (blood loss, drug levels), integrals add up the total (fluid lost, dose given), and optimization finds the best design (less material, less waste, more safety).

PROBLEM 1 · INTEGRALS

How much blood has the patient lost?

During surgery, bleeding is fastest soon after the incision and then slows as vessels are controlled. The anesthesia team and I track the rate (mL/min), but what matters is the total lost, which is the area under the rate curve.
R(t) = A · t · e−t/b   →   Total = ∫0T R(t) dt
Peak rate
Total loss
Show the solution step by step

Step 1. Total loss is the integral of the rate. Use integration by parts with u = t, dv = e−t/b dt.

∫ t e−t/b dt = −b t e−t/b − b2 e−t/b + C

Step 2. Apply the limits from 0 to T and multiply by A.

Total = A b2 [ 1 − e−T/b (1 + T/b) ]

Step 3. The peak happens where R′(t) = 0. R′(t) = A e−t/b(1 − t/b), so t = b and Rmax = A b / e.

On the job: This is why sponge and suction-canister counts matter. Adding up small rates over time is exactly what an integral does.
PROBLEM 2 · DERIVATIVES

How fast is the anesthetic wearing off?

Drug concentration in the blood falls exponentially. The derivative tells us how quickly the level is dropping at any moment, which affects when a patient can safely wake up.
C(t) = C0 e−kt,   k = ln 2 / (half-life)  →  C′(t) = −k C(t)
Level C(t)
Rate C′(t) mg/L/min
Time until level drops to 1 mg/L (wake-up threshold)
Show the solution step by step

Step 1. Half-life means C drops to half: C0/2 = C0 e−kh, so k = ln 2 / h.

Step 2. Differentiate with the chain rule.

C′(t) = −k C0 e−kt = −k C(t)

Step 3. The drop is proportional to the level, so it is fastest at the start and slows down. The orange tangent line on the graph has slope C′(t). Solve C(t) = 1 for the threshold time: t = ln(C0) / k.

On the job: Knowing the rate of change helps the team time reversal agents and predict recovery, instead of guessing.
PROBLEM 3 · OPTIMIZATION

Designing the best sterilization canister

A cylindrical instrument canister must hold a fixed volume V. Less surface area means less material, less weight to carry, and less surface to sterilize. What radius uses the least material?
V = π r2 h  →  SA(r) = 2πr2 + 2V/r
Surface area at your r
Height h
Optimal radius r*
Minimum surface area
Show the solution step by step

Step 1. Solve the volume for h: h = V / (π r2), then substitute to get SA(r) = 2πr2 + 2V/r.

Step 2. Differentiate and set equal to zero.

SA′(r) = 4πr − 2V/r2 = 0  →  r* = (V / 2π)1/3

Step 3. Second derivative test: SA″(r) = 4π + 4V/r3 > 0, so this is a minimum.

Step 4. At r*, h = V/(πr*2) = 2r*. The most efficient canister has height equal to its diameter.

On the job: Less material and less surface means lower cost, easier cleaning, and a lighter, easier-to-handle tray in a busy OR.
